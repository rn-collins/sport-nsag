The Six Assessment Dimensions

Every NSAG assessment has six dimensions — specific, observable, documentable governance criteria. For SPORT, those six dimensions are:

D1

Physical Environment Governance

Are the physical spaces in which athletes train, compete, and recover governed by documented evidence-based standards? Do facility design decisions — acoustic environment, lighting, thermal conditions, nature access, spatial configuration — have documented evidence rationale and post-occupancy evaluation criteria?

Why it matters: Athletic facility design is treated as an aesthetic and functional question. It is a governance question. The same built environment evidence base that applies to healthcare facilities (NSAG M10) applies here with equal force and less institutional awareness.

D2

Schedule and Recovery Governance

Are training loads, competition schedules, and recovery windows governed by documented standards that specify the evidence base for those standards? Is there a documented framework for distinguishing adaptive stress from impairing stress — and does the organization measure which it is producing?

Why it matters: Athletic governance that mandates training frequency without mandating recovery standards is governing only one side of the nervous system equation. Load and recovery are a single physiological unit. Separate governance for each produces systematic allostatic load accumulation that organizations cannot see because they are measuring only one variable.

D3

Travel and Logistical Stress Governance

Are the cognitive and physiological effects of travel — sleep disruption, chronobiological disruption, environmental stressor accumulation — documented and governed? Are travel decisions that affect athlete nervous system function made with reference to evidence on those effects?

Why it matters: Sleep restriction in athletic populations produces cognitive performance impairment before physical performance impairment. An organization making travel decisions on budget and logistical grounds alone is making nervous system governance decisions invisibly. SPORT assessments make those effects visible and documented.

D4

Psychological Safety and Organizational Culture

Is the psychological environment in which athletes operate — the communication norms, accountability culture, power structure, and trust infrastructure — assessed against documented standards for psychological safety? Is psychological safety treated as a governance variable with measurable outcomes, not as a culture initiative?

Why it matters: Psychological threat activates the same physiological stress response as physical threat. An organizational culture that produces chronic threat activation in athletes is producing measurable nervous system effects that directly impair the performance and wellbeing outcomes the organization claims to want.

D5

Media and Public-Facing Stress Governance

Are the cognitive and psychological demands of media obligations — attention, emotional performance, public exposure, social media — governed? Is there a documented framework for what the organization requires of athletes in public-facing contexts and what recovery from those demands looks like?

Why it matters: Media and public exposure are not soft factors in athletic governance. They produce real allostatic load that accumulates alongside training load. Organizations that govern training load rigorously but impose media obligations without governance are producing cumulative stress effects they are not measuring.

D6

Development and Career Transition Governance

Does the organization's governance framework account for the distinct neurobiological contexts of athlete development stages — youth development, peak performance years, career transition, and post-athletic life? Are governance standards calibrated to developmental stage, not just chronological age?

Why it matters: The nervous system demands of athletic development are not constant across a career. Youth athletes in nervous system development periods, elite athletes at peak allostatic load, and transitioning athletes facing identity restructuring have distinct governance needs. A single framework applied without developmental differentiation is systematically misaligned with the population it governs.

NSAG Principles in This Domain

The Athlete's Nervous System Audit is not a standalone product — it deploys specific NSAG principles that shape every dimension of the assessment.

P1

Institutions regulate nervous system states — always.

Sports organizations make hundreds of decisions that directly regulate athlete nervous system states — scheduling, environment, travel, recovery culture, media exposure. Most have no governance framework for those effects. SPORT makes this invisible governance visible and intentional.

P7

Threat and safety are institutional products.

Organizational culture in sports produces threat or safety — through communication norms, accountability practices, power structures, and competitive pressure design. These are not soft factors. They are physiological inputs that shape athlete nervous system states and, through those states, performance and wellbeing outcomes.

P9

Chronic stress produces institutional incapacity.

Burnout, injury, attrition, and ethical failure in athletic institutions are not random. They are institutional design outputs. The evidence on stress physiology, allostatic load, and performance is decades old. SPORT assessments connect this evidence to the governance decisions that produce or prevent those outcomes.

P13

Agency is a design variable.

An athlete who cannot influence their training environment, travel schedule, or recovery protocol does not have the agency required for the performance and wellbeing outcomes athletic institutions claim to want. Agency governance is not athlete empowerment as a value — it is a performance variable with documented evidence.

Signal vs. Noise

What Is Worth Paying Attention To

Signal

Sleep restriction research in elite athletes (2020-2024)

Controlled studies on sleep restriction in athletic populations have established that cognitive performance impairment precedes physical performance impairment — meaning athletes are making lower-quality decisions under conditions that still appear physically functional. This has direct governance implications for travel scheduling and early-morning training practices. Primary source: Sleep Medicine Reviews, Frontiers in Sports and Active Living.

Signal

NCAA mental health framework implementation studies

The NCAA mental health framework adoption data provides comparative evidence on which organizational governance changes produce measurable athlete mental health outcomes versus which produce compliance documentation without outcome change. The gap between these categories is large. Primary source: NCAA Sport Science Institute annual reports.

Noise

Load monitoring technology vendor claims

Wearable and GPS load monitoring vendors publish case studies asserting that their technology improves athlete welfare outcomes. These studies are funded and designed by the vendors and should not be treated as independent evidence. The technology may be useful; the vendor evidence base is not the appropriate source for governance decisions.

Noise

Athlete mental health awareness campaigns as governance

Awareness campaigns and stigma-reduction initiatives are communication interventions. They do not constitute governance frameworks. An organization can have excellent mental health communication and simultaneously have scheduling, travel, and environment practices that systematically impair athlete nervous system function.

Terminology

Sport NSAG Glossary

Language used precisely in this domain. These distinctions carry real analytical weight.

Allostatic load

The cumulative burden of chronic stress and life events on the body and nervous system. In athletes, allostatic load accumulates across training stress, competition stress, travel, media obligations, and life outside sport. Governance frameworks that only measure training load are measuring one input to allostatic load while ignoring the others.

Adaptive stress vs. impairing stress

Adaptive stress is the stimulus that drives physical and cognitive development — the appropriate application of load relative to recovery capacity. Impairing stress exceeds recovery capacity and degrades function over time. The distinction is not about intensity but about the relationship between load and recovery. Governing bodies that mandate training frequency without mandating recovery standards are governing only one side of this equation.

Cognitive performance impairment

Reduction in an individual's capacity for attention, decision-making, emotional regulation, and executive function. In athletes, cognitive performance impairment typically precedes measurable physical performance impairment under sleep restriction and overtraining conditions — meaning organizations can be producing cognitively impaired athletes while performance metrics appear stable.

Nervous system recovery

The process by which the autonomic nervous system returns to parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) dominance after periods of sympathetic (fight-or-flight) activation. Adequate nervous system recovery is distinct from muscular recovery and requires different inputs — primarily sleep quality, psychological safety, and absence of chronic stressors. Many high-performance environments optimize for muscular recovery while inadvertently preventing nervous system recovery.

Athlete welfare governance

The documented policies, assessment processes, and decision-making frameworks through which a sports organization manages its obligation to the cognitive, psychological, and physical integrity of the athletes in its care. Distinct from athlete welfare communication (statements about values) and athlete welfare programming (wellness initiatives). Governance is the structural accountability layer.
